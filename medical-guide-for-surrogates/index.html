The medical side of surrogacy, explained

From screening to embryo transfer and pregnancy monitoring — here's what happens medically, and why each step matters.

See if you qualify

Medical note: This page is educational only and is not medical advice. Screening, medications, pregnancy risks, and eligibility vary by clinic and individual health history. Always follow guidance from your fertility clinic and OB.

Medical screening and clearance

Before you are cleared for a surrogacy journey, you’ll go through agency pre-screening and fertility clinic medical evaluation. Some screening may happen before matching, while full clinic clearance often happens after a potential match.

Clinic evaluation commonly includes a physical exam, uterine evaluation such as ultrasound or saline sonogram, bloodwork, infectious disease screening, review of pregnancy records, and psychological evaluation with a licensed mental health professional.

Medications and the IVF cycle

Clinic protocols and monitoring may vary, but embryo-transfer medication and embryo transfer generally require legal clearance and a fully executed Gestational Carrier Agreement. Once cleared, your clinic prepares your cycle for embryo transfer. This may include:

Cycle regulation

Birth control pills or other timing tools may be used briefly to coordinate your cycle.

Estrogen support

Estrogen helps build the uterine lining to the right thickness for implantation, usually by patch, pill, or injection depending on clinic protocol.

Progesterone support

Progesterone is commonly used leading up to and after transfer. It may involve injections, vaginal medication, or another protocol.

Monitoring visits

Ultrasounds and bloodwork help the clinic track your body's readiness in real time.

"The medication plan can sound intimidating at first, but your clinic will teach you exactly what to do and when to do it."

Embryo transfer day

This is usually a short outpatient procedure. A thin catheter places the embryo directly into your uterus, guided by ultrasound. Anesthesia is usually not required. Your clinic will give specific instructions about rest and activity afterward.

The two-week wait and confirmation

About 9–14 days after transfer, a blood test confirms whether pregnancy has started. If positive, you will usually have follow-up bloodwork to confirm hormone levels are rising appropriately, followed by an ultrasound around 6–7 weeks.

Pregnancy and prenatal care

Once the pregnancy is confirmed and stable, care typically transitions from the fertility clinic to an OB. The pregnancy is usually managed much like any other pregnancy, while the surrogacy journey still includes coordination with the fertility clinic, agency, attorneys, and intended parents.

Common medical questions

Will I gain weight from the medications?

Some surrogates notice temporary bloating or water retention from hormones. The protocol is not the same as egg-retrieval IVF medications, but individual experiences vary.

What if the first transfer doesn't work?

It happens, and it is not a reflection of anything you did. Your clinic will review the cycle and may plan another transfer attempt if more embryos are available and everyone agrees to continue.

Is the pregnancy itself any different from a regular pregnancy?

Once established, the pregnancy is usually managed much like any other pregnancy, but the surrogacy journey includes IVF/FET medications, extra screening, legal clearance, and coordination with the fertility clinic, agency, and intended parents.
